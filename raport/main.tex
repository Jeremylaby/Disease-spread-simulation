\section{\SectionTitleIntroduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modeling and simulation of disease spread in populations is an important research area, especially in the context of public health and epidemic control. Various approaches have been proposed in the literature, including classical compartmental models such as SIR and SEIR~\cite{kermack1927sir, hethcote2000mathematics}, stochastic models~\cite{allen2017primer}, and more advanced approaches such as agent-based simulations that aim to capture individual behavior and interactions.

One of the key challenges in this domain is the trade-off between model simplicity and realism. While simple models are computationally efficient and provide useful analytical insights, they often rely on strong assumptions that do not fully reflect real-world conditions. In particular, they typically assume homogeneous mixing of the population and limited variability in individual behavior, which can lead to discrepancies between simulated and real epidemic dynamics.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Citations}
\label{sec:citations}

Classical approaches to epidemic modeling are based on compartmental models, such as the SIR model introduced by Kermack and McKendrick~\cite{kermack1927sir}, and its extensions, including the SEIR model discussed in~\cite{hethcote2000mathematics}. These models provide a theoretical foundation for analyzing the dynamics of infectious diseases.

More advanced approaches include stochastic models, which incorporate randomness into the simulation of infection and recovery processes~\cite{allen2017primer}, and agent-based models, which explicitly represent individuals and their interactions, allowing for the inclusion of heterogeneous behavior and contact structures~\cite{cdc2025transmission}.

In addition to traditional mathematical models, researchers have also explored alternative approaches to understanding epidemic dynamics through virtual environments. An interesting example is the analysis of the "Corrupted Blood" incident in an online game~\cite{lofgren2007virtual}. In this case, a bug led to the uncontrolled spread of a disease-like effect among players, resembling real-world epidemic behavior.

Studies such as~\cite{lofgren2007virtual} suggest that virtual environments can provide valuable insights into the spread of infectious diseases and human behavioral responses. Similar ideas appear in designed simulation environments such as the game Plague Inc., which incorporates epidemiological concepts such as the basic reproduction number and global transmission networks~\cite{cornellPlagueInc2014, gideonR02024}. At the same time, these approaches highlight important limitations, particularly related to unrealistic behavior and the lack of real-world consequences, which may significantly affect the dynamics of the system.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Lists}
\label{sec:lists}

Several approaches to modeling the spread of diseases in populations can be distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Compartmental models} (e.g., SIR, SEIR), which divide the population into groups and describe transitions between them using mathematical equations. These models form the theoretical foundation of epidemic modeling.

\item \textbf{Stochastic models}, which introduce randomness into the
  simulation by treating infections and recoveries as probabilistic
  events, producing different epidemic trajectories on each run.

\item \textbf{Agent-based models}, where each individual is simulated separately, allowing for a more realistic representation of interactions, heterogeneity, and behavioral factors.

\item \textbf{Virtual environment simulations}, such as the "Corrupted Blood" incident in online games, which provide insight into emergent behavior and the role of human decisions in epidemic dynamics.
\end{itemize}

These approaches differ in terms of complexity, realism, and computational cost. In particular, studies based on virtual environments indicate that human behavior can significantly influence the dynamics of disease spread, which is often not fully captured by classical models.

In the following section, selected modeling approaches are discussed in more detail, including classical compartmental models (SIR and SEIR) as well as observations derived from virtual epidemic scenarios.

\subsection{Modeling Approaches}
\label{sec:modeling-approaches}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsubsection{SIR Model}
\label{sec:sir}

The SIR model, introduced by Kermack and McKendrick~\cite{kermack1927sir}, is one of the most fundamental approaches to modeling the spread of infectious diseases. It divides the population into three compartments: susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered (R).

The model describes the transitions between these groups over time. Susceptible individuals become infected through contact with infected individuals, and infected individuals eventually recover (or are removed from the system). The dynamics of the model are governed by two main parameters: the infection rate and the recovery rate.

A key insight of the SIR model is that an epidemic does not continue indefinitely. As the number of susceptible individuals decreases, the disease gradually loses its ability to spread, leading to a decline in the number of infected individuals. This explains why epidemics often peak and then subside even without infecting the entire population.

From the modeling perspective, the SIR model is simple and computationally efficient, making it a useful baseline for simulations. However, it assumes homogeneous mixing of the population and does not account for individual behavior or contact structure, which limits its realism in more complex scenarios.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsubsection{SEIR Model}
\label{sec:seir}

The SEIR model is an extension of the classical SIR framework and is discussed in the broader review of infectious disease models by Hethcote~\cite{hethcote2000mathematics}. In this model, the population is divided into four compartments: susceptible (S), exposed (E), infected (I), and recovered (R).

The key difference between the SIR and SEIR models is the introduction of the exposed class. This compartment contains individuals who have already been infected but are not yet infectious. Such a formulation makes the model more appropriate for diseases with a non-negligible latent or incubation period.

From the perspective of modeling and simulation, the SEIR model provides a more realistic description of epidemic dynamics than the basic SIR model. In particular, it allows the simulation to represent the delay between infection and infectiousness, which may significantly affect the timing and shape of the epidemic peak.

At the same time, the SEIR model remains relatively simple and computationally tractable. However, similarly to the SIR model, it still relies on simplifying assumptions such as homogeneous mixing of the population and limited representation of individual behavior.

\subsubsection{Other Compartmental Model Variants}
\label{sec:variants}

The SIR and SEIR models serve as a foundation for a number of variants
that introduce additional compartments to capture specific aspects of
disease dynamics~\cite{hethcote2000mathematics}. The most common
extensions include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SIS} -- removes the recovered class; individuals return to
  the susceptible state after infection, modeling diseases where
  reinfection is possible (e.g.\ sexually transmitted infections),
\item \textbf{SIRD} -- splits the recovered class into recovered and
  deceased, allowing the model to track mortality separately,
\item \textbf{MSIR} -- adds a maternally immune class at birth,
  representing temporary immunity inherited from the mother,
\item \textbf{SIR-V / SEIR-V} -- adds a vaccinated class, enabling the
  evaluation of vaccination campaign effects on epidemic spread.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
All of these variants follow the same principle of transitions between
compartments, differing only in the number and type of groups considered.

\subsubsection{Stochastic Approach to Epidemic Modeling}
\label{sec:stochastic}

The SIR and SEIR models described above are deterministic -- given the
same parameters and initial conditions, they always produce the same
epidemic curve. In each time step of fixed length, the model computes new
values of $S$, $I$, and $R$ using differential equations, which may
result in fractional numbers of individuals (e.g.\ 3.7~infected persons).
While this is mathematically convenient, it does not reflect reality:
a~person either becomes infected or does not, and these events do not
occur at regular intervals.

Stochastic epidemic models address this by simulating individual events
rather than computing continuous changes. One of the most widely used methods for this purpose is the Gillespie algorithm, originally developed for chemical kinetics~\cite{gillespie1977exact} and later widely adopted in computational epidemiology~\cite{allen2017primer}.

First, the time until the next event is drawn from an exponential
distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \tau = \frac{-\ln(r_1)}{a_{\text{total}}}\,,
  \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
where $r_1$ is a random number from the interval $(0,1)$ and
$a_{\text{total}}$ is the total rate of all possible events, defined as:
\begin{equation}
  a_{\text{total}} = \frac{\beta\, S\, I}{N} + \gamma\, I\,.
  \label{eq:a-total}
\end{equation}
Here $\beta$ is the infection rate, representing how quickly the disease
spreads through contact between susceptible and infected individuals,
and $\gamma$ is the recovery rate, representing how quickly infected
individuals recover. A~higher value of~$\beta$ (more contagious disease)
or a~lower value of~$\gamma$ (longer recovery time) both increase
$a_{\text{total}}$, which in turn decreases~$\tau$ -- meaning that
events occur more frequently and the epidemic progresses faster.

Second, the algorithm determines whether the event is an infection or
a~recovery. Another random number~$r_2$ is drawn from $(0,1)$ and
compared with a threshold:
\begin{equation}
  \text{event} =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{infection}  & \text{if } r_2 < \dfrac{\beta\, S\, I / N}
                        {a_{\text{total}}}\,,\\[6pt]
    \text{recovery}   & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:event-selection}
\end{equation}
In each step, exactly one individual changes state: either one person
moves from~$S$ to~$I$, or one person moves from~$I$ to~$R$. The
simulation clock advances by~$\tau$, and the procedure repeats until no
infected individuals remain.

Because of the random draws, each run of the simulation produces
a~different epidemic trajectory. This is particularly important for small
populations or the early stages of an outbreak, where a single random
event -- such as the first infected person recovering before infecting
anyone else -- can determine whether an epidemic occurs at
all~\cite{allen2017primer}.

\subsubsection{Agent-Based Models}
\label{sec:abm}

Agent-based models (ABMs) build upon and extend the network-based
approach to epidemic modeling. In contrast to compartmental models, where
every individual has an equal chance of contact with every other
individual, ABMs represent the population as a network of connections --
each person can only interact with a limited set of neighbors, such as
family members, coworkers, or people in close physical proximity. A
susceptible individual with $k_i$ infected neighbors has a probability of
becoming infected.



In an agent-based system, each node in this network becomes an autonomous
\emph{agent} representing a single individual. An agent carries its own
internal state -- health status (susceptible, infected, or recovered),
age, sex, and optionally a position on a spatial grid or map. Crucially,
each agent also has its own decision logic that determines how it behaves
at each simulation step: whether it moves, how it interacts with
neighbors, and how its state changes over time~\cite{cdc2025transmission}.
For example, a healthy agent may be programmed to move away from visibly
infected neighbors, while an infected agent may remain stationary. The
parameters governing state transitions can also vary between agents --
older agents may have a higher probability of infection or a longer
recovery time, reflecting the heterogeneity observed in real populations.

The agent-based approach is not limited to modeling individual persons.
The system can also include agents representing elements of the
environment, such as hospitals that accept infected agents within their
area and increase their recovery rate, subject to a limited capacity.

The most important distinguishing feature of this approach is that the
overall epidemic curve is not computed from input parameters using
equations. Instead, it emerges during the simulation from the sum of
local interactions between agents. This means that the same model can be
used to interactively test different scenarios on the simulated
population -- such as introducing quarantine measures, changing contact
patterns, or adding vaccination -- and observe how the epidemic dynamics
change in response. ABMs also make it possible to trace the full chain
of transmission, identifying which agent infected which, and to detect
phenomena such as superspreaders that arise naturally from the structure
of the contact network~\cite{cdc2025transmission}.

However, this flexibility comes at a cost. Agent-based models are more
computationally demanding than compartmental models, require more data to
parameterize, and are generally slower to design and
run~\cite{cdc2025transmission}. For this reason, compartmental models
remain useful for rapid initial assessments, while ABMs are better suited
for detailed analysis once sufficient data about the population and
disease characteristics are available.
\subsection{Virtual Epidemics in Online Environments}
\label{sec:wow-epidemic}

\subsubsection{The Corrupted Blood Incident}
\label{sec:corrupted-blood}

An interesting case related to modeling epidemic dynamics is the so-called "Corrupted Blood" incident observed in the game \cite{lofgren2007virtual}. In this case, a bug introduced a disease-like effect that could spread between players, leading to an uncontrolled epidemic within the virtual environment.

From the perspective of modeling and simulation, this incident is particularly valuable because it revealed emergent behavior that was not explicitly designed by the developers. The spread of the disease depended not only on the mechanics of the system, but also on the actions of individual players, which introduced a level of unpredictability similar to real-world scenarios.

Several phenomena observed during this event resemble real epidemic dynamics. These include rapid spread in densely populated areas, the presence of individuals who acted as "super-spreaders", and attempts by some players to avoid infection by isolating themselves. At the same time, a significant number of players deliberately contributed to the spread, which highlights the importance of behavioral factors in epidemic modeling.

However, as discussed in \cite{lofgren2007virtual}, such virtual environments also exhibit important limitations. In particular, player behavior is influenced by the lack of real-world consequences, such as health risks or economic costs. This leads to actions that would be unlikely in real populations, including intentional exposure to the disease or reckless movement patterns.

From the modeling perspective, this case illustrates a key challenge in simulation of complex systems: accurately capturing human behavior. While classical models such as SIR or SEIR focus primarily on aggregate dynamics, real-world systems are strongly affected by individual decisions and interactions. Therefore, incorporating behavioral aspects into simulation models is crucial for improving their realism and predictive capabilities.

The "Corrupted Blood" incident can thus be treated as an illustrative example showing both the potential and the limitations of using simulated environments for studying epidemic spread.

\subsubsection{Plague Inc.}
\label{sec:plague-inc}

Plague Inc. is a strategy-simulation mobile game developed by Ndemic
Creations in 2012, in which the player controls a pathogen with the goal
of infecting and eliminating the global population. Unlike the Corrupted
Blood incident discussed above, Plague Inc. was designed intentionally as
an epidemic simulation, and its mechanics are rooted in epidemiological
concepts~\cite{cornellPlagueInc2014}.

The game does not implement any specific academic model. According to its
creator James Vaughan, the core design is based on the concept of the
basic reproduction number~($R_0$) -- a metric that represents the
average number of secondary infections caused by a single infected
individual in a fully susceptible
population~\cite{gideonR02024}. The central game mechanic revolves
around the so-called ``Infection Cycle'', which determines how
individuals become infected and how they transmit the disease to
others~\cite{cornellPlagueInc2014}.

In practice, the game combines elements of several modeling approaches.
Populations of individual countries transition through health states
(healthy, infected, deceased) in a manner similar to compartmental
models. Global air and sea transport networks create connections between
countries, reflecting a network-based approach to disease spread.
Additionally, in-game governments react to the epidemic by closing
borders and developing a cure, introducing a behavioral component.
Disease can also be transmitted by animals, and global events such as the
Olympics create temporary connections between populations of different
countries, accelerating the spread of the
pathogen~\cite{cornellPlagueInc2014}. Despite these
realistic elements, Ndemic Creations has stated that the game is based on
a scientific understanding of infectious disease spread but should not be
considered a scientific model~\cite{cornellPlagueInc2014}.

\section{\SectionTitleProblemSolution}
\label{sec:solution}

The research problem addressed in this project is the simulation of
disease spread in a heterogeneous population. The goal is not only to
reproduce the general shape of an epidemic curve, but also to represent
how local contacts, individual behavior, vaccination, hospitals, and
quarantine-like restrictions influence the dynamics of the epidemic. In
contrast to purely compartmental models, where the population is
described only by aggregate variables, the proposed solution uses an
agent-based model. Each person is represented as an individual agent with
its own state, age, position, vaccination status, and movement behavior.
This makes it possible to observe epidemic dynamics that emerge from
local interactions rather than being directly imposed by differential
equations.

The model follows the SEIRD structure. Each citizen belongs to one of
five epidemiological states: susceptible ($S$), exposed ($E$), infected
($I$), recovered ($R$), or deceased ($D$). Susceptible agents may become
exposed after contact with infected neighbors. Exposed agents represent
individuals in the incubation period; after a fixed number of simulation
steps they become infected. Infected agents may recover, die, or move
toward a hospital if one is nearby. Recovered agents may either keep
permanent immunity or lose immunity after a configured number of steps.
Deceased agents are removed from the spatial grid and no longer
participate in the simulation.

The simulation is implemented in Python using the Mesa~\cite{mesaDocs2026}
framework for agent-based modeling. Mesa provides the basic
abstractions for agents, models, and spatial environments. The
population is placed on a
two-dimensional \texttt{MultiGrid}, where multiple agents may occupy the
same cell. The project uses Python dataclasses to keep the global
configuration explicit and easy to modify, pandas~\cite{pandasDocs2026}
to store collected simulation results, and
Matplotlib~\cite{matplotlibDocs2026} to visualize
both the final epidemic curve and the live spatial state of the
simulation. The most important configurable
parameters are the population size, grid dimensions,
infection probability, recovery probability, incubation period, mortality
parameters, hospital capacity, vaccination effectiveness, avoidance
behavior, immunity duration, quarantine threshold, and number of
simulation steps.

The system provides two execution modes. The standard script,
\texttt{run.py}, runs the model for a configured number of steps and then
plots the aggregated SEIRD curves. The interactive script,
\texttt{run\_live.py}, displays the grid during the simulation. It shows
citizens as colored points according to their current epidemiological
state, marks hospitals and vaccination points with separate symbols, and
adds a legend with the current state counts. The live mode accepts a
command-line argument expressed in hundredths of a second; for example,
\texttt{python run\_live.py 50} updates the displayed frame every half
second.

\subsection{Model Initialization}
\label{sec:solution-initialization}

At the beginning of the simulation, the model creates three types of
objects: citizens, hospitals, and vaccination points. Hospitals and
vaccination points are placed at fixed positions defined in the
configuration. Citizens are generated with randomly sampled ages based on
predefined age brackets and their corresponding probabilities. A
configured number of citizens is initially assigned to the infected
state, while the rest start as susceptible. A selected fraction of the
population is marked as ``avoiders'', meaning that these agents try to
avoid cells occupied by visibly infected individuals.

The model uses a random seed to make simulation runs reproducible. This
is important because infection, recovery, death, movement, and age
sampling all contain stochastic elements. With the same seed and the same
configuration, the same simulation trajectory can be recreated.

\subsection{Agent Behavior and Movement}
\label{sec:solution-agent-behavior}

During each simulation step, all agents are activated in random order.
This random activation avoids a fixed update order and better reflects
the stochastic nature of the modeled process. Citizen agents first decide
whether and where to move, and then update their epidemiological state.

Movement is local and takes place on the grid neighborhood of the
agent's current position. Younger agents move normally, while agents
above a configured age threshold have a lower probability of moving in a
given step. This represents reduced mobility among older individuals.
Avoider agents inspect neighboring cells and prefer destinations without
infected citizens and outside quarantine zones. If no safe cell is
available, they fall back to the available neighboring cells. Infected
agents behave differently: if a hospital is within a configured search
radius, they move toward the nearest hospital and prefer cells that bring
them closer to it.

\subsection{Infection Algorithm}
\label{sec:solution-infection}

The infection process is based on local contacts. For each susceptible
citizen, the model checks neighboring cells and counts the number of
infected citizens in the neighborhood. Let $k$ denote the number of
infected neighbors and let $\beta$ denote the base transmission
probability per infected contact. The probability that the susceptible
agent becomes exposed in a given step is computed as:

\begin{equation}
P(\mathrm{infection}) = 1 - (1 - \beta)^k.
\end{equation}

This formula assumes that each infected neighbor creates an independent
chance of transmission. If there are no infected neighbors, the infection
probability is zero. If the agent is vaccinated, the effective
transmission probability is reduced according to the vaccination
effectiveness parameter:

\begin{equation}
\beta_{\mathrm{vaccinated}} = \beta \cdot (1 - e_v),
\end{equation}

where $e_v$ is the configured vaccination effectiveness. This means that
vaccination does not create complete immunity by default, but decreases
the probability of infection during contact with infected agents.

\subsection{Disease Progression}
\label{sec:solution-disease-progression}

Disease progression is modeled as a finite-state process. When a
susceptible citizen becomes infected, the agent first enters the exposed
state. The exposed state lasts for a fixed incubation period measured in
simulation steps. After the incubation timer reaches zero, the agent
becomes infected and can start spreading the disease to others.

In each step, an infected agent may recover with probability $\gamma$.
This base recovery probability can be increased by a hospital recovery
bonus if the agent is inside the area of a hospital that still has
available capacity. The effective recovery probability is therefore:

\begin{equation}
\gamma_{\mathrm{effective}} =
\begin{cases}
\gamma + b_h, & \text{if the agent is inside a non-full hospital area,}\\
\gamma, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $b_h$ is the configured hospital recovery bonus. For the default
configuration, $\gamma = 0.05$ and $b_h = 0.05$, so an infected agent in
range of a hospital with available capacity has a recovery probability
of $0.10$ in a simulation step instead of $0.05$. If recovery does not
occur, the model checks whether the agent dies. Mortality is
age-dependent: all infected agents have a base mortality probability,
and an additional age-related penalty is added for agents above the
configured age threshold. This makes older agents more vulnerable, which
improves the realism of the population model.

Recovered agents receive immunity. If the configured immunity duration is
zero, immunity is treated as permanent. Otherwise, the immunity timer is
decreased in each step and the agent eventually returns to the
susceptible state. This allows the model to represent both permanent and
temporary immunity scenarios.

\subsection{Environmental Objects and Interventions}
\label{sec:solution-interventions}

Hospitals and vaccination points are modeled as fixed environmental
agents. A hospital has a position, area radius, capacity, and recovery
bonus. During each step, it counts infected citizens within its area. If
the number of patients does not exceed the capacity, infected citizens in
the hospital area receive an increased chance of recovery. Once the
hospital reaches capacity, the recovery bonus is no longer applied.

Vaccination points also have fixed positions. When a susceptible citizen
enters a cell containing a vaccination point, the citizen becomes
vaccinated. This vaccination status then reduces the effective infection
probability in future contacts according to
$\beta_{\mathrm{vaccinated}} = \beta \cdot (1 - e_v)$, where $e_v$ is
the vaccination effectiveness. In this model, vaccination is represented
as a preventive mechanism rather than a treatment for already infected
agents.

The model also includes a simple quarantine mechanism. If the number of
infected citizens exceeds a configured threshold, quarantine zones are
created around positions occupied by infected agents. These zones
restrict the movement choices of healthy avoider agents and random-moving
agents. The mechanism is intentionally simple, but it introduces a global
response to the epidemic and allows the simulation to represent limited
mobility under epidemic pressure.

\subsection{Live Spatial Visualization}
\label{sec:solution-live-visualization}

In addition to the final epidemic curve, the project includes a live
visualization of the spatial simulation. This view is implemented in
\texttt{run\_live.py} using the interactive plotting mode of
Matplotlib~\cite{matplotlibDocs2026}. Each frame renders the current grid
state: susceptible, exposed, infected, recovered, and deceased citizens
are represented with different colors.
Vaccinated citizens are additionally marked with an outline, so the
effect of vaccination points can be observed directly on the grid.
Hospitals are drawn with a distinct marker and a visible radius of
influence, while vaccination points are displayed with their own marker.
The side panel contains a legend and live counters for all SEIRD states,
the number of vaccinated citizens, and the number of active quarantine
zones.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/live_simulation.png}
  \caption{Live spatial visualization of the agent-based epidemic
  simulation. Citizens are displayed on the grid according to their
  current epidemiological state, while hospitals and vaccination points
  are marked with separate symbols.}
  \label{fig:live-simulation}
\end{figure}

The live visualization is based on snapshots of the model state. A
snapshot stores the current step number, agent positions, epidemiological
states, vaccination flags, hospital occupancy, vaccination point
statistics, and quarantine zones. The graphical interface displays the
snapshot for step $n$, while the next step, $n+1$, is computed in the
background by a single-worker \texttt{ThreadPoolExecutor}. This separates
simulation computation from frame rendering and reduces visible pauses in
the animation. When the configured delay passes, the already computed
snapshot is displayed as the next frame.

\subsection{Data Collection and Visualization}
\label{sec:solution-data-visualization}

The model uses Mesa's \texttt{DataCollector} to record the number of
agents in each epidemiological state at every simulation step. The
collected variables are susceptible, exposed, infected, recovered,
deceased, vaccinated, and the number of active quarantine zones. After
the simulation ends, the collected data are converted to a pandas
\texttt{DataFrame}. In the standard execution mode, the epidemic curve is
visualized using Matplotlib by plotting the SEIRD state counts over time.
In the live execution mode, the same underlying model is advanced step by
step, but the current spatial state is rendered continuously before the
final aggregate plot is needed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/epidemic_curve.png}
  \caption{Aggregated epidemic curve produced after running the standard
  simulation. The plot shows the number of susceptible, exposed,
  infected, recovered, and deceased agents over time.}
  \label{fig:epidemic-curve}
\end{figure}

The simulation can stop before reaching the maximum number of steps if no
infected or exposed agents remain. In that case, the epidemic is treated
as finished. Otherwise, the model runs until the configured number of
steps is reached. The resulting plots provide a direct way to analyze how
the number of susceptible, exposed, infected, recovered, and deceased
agents changes during the simulated epidemic.

\section{\SectionTitleExperiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:experiments-protocol}

The purpose of the experiments was to determine how selected disease
parameters and intervention mechanisms influence the epidemic curve. The
experiments were performed without the live visualization, using the same
agent-based simulation model described in Section~\ref{sec:solution}. The
baseline scenario used a population of 500 citizens on a $50 \times 50$
grid, with five initially infected agents, $\beta = 0.30$,
$\gamma = 0.05$, permanent post-recovery immunity, one hospital, and two
vaccination points.

Eleven scenarios were tested: the baseline configuration and ten modified
configurations grouped into transmission, vaccination, hospital,
behavioral, and quarantine experiments. Each scenario was simulated ten
times, with seeds from 1000 to 1009. The same set of seeds was used for
each scenario in order to limit the influence of stochastic variability
on comparisons. The maximum simulation horizon was 1000 steps; however,
all 110 runs ended earlier because no exposed or infected agents
remained.

The simulation state was saved after every step. In total, the
experiments generated 25,827 step-level observations. A validation of the
saved results confirmed that the sum of susceptible, exposed, infected,
recovered, and deceased citizens remained equal to 500 in every recorded
state. The complete raw data, summaries, and generated plots are stored
in \path{chapter_3/results/chapter3_full_10_replicates}.

The following measures were used in the analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{peak infected} -- the largest number of simultaneously
  infected citizens during a run,
\item \textbf{attack rate} -- the percentage of the initial population
  that became infected at any point in a run,
\item \textbf{final deceased} -- the number of deceased citizens at the
  end of a run,
\item \textbf{duration} -- the step at which no exposed or infected
  citizens remained.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Transmission Probability}
\label{sec:experiments-transmission}

Changing the transmission probability produced the expected change in
the epidemic scale. Reducing $\beta$ from 0.30 to 0.15 decreased the
mean peak number of infected citizens from 44.4 to 28.0 and decreased
the attack rate by 24.04 percentage points relative to the baseline.
The mean number of deceased citizens fell from 51.8 to 29.5. Increasing
$\beta$ to 0.50 resulted in a peak of 63.2 infected citizens, an attack
rate of 66.8\%, and 60.2 deceased citizens on average. Thus, within
this model, transmission probability is a strong determinant of both
epidemic magnitude and mortality.

\subsection{Vaccination Experiment}
\label{sec:experiments-vaccination}

Removing the two default vaccination points changed the baseline outcome
only slightly: the attack rate was 56.6\%, compared with 56.8\% in the
baseline scenario. This result is consistent with the small number of
agents vaccinated in the baseline, equal to 24.3 on average. The two
default points therefore provide limited coverage in a population of
500 moving agents.

The expanded vaccination programme used 17 distributed vaccination
points and increased vaccine effectiveness from 0.70 to 0.90. In this
scenario, 174.7 citizens were vaccinated on average. Compared with the
scenario without vaccination points, the mean peak decreased by 13.4
citizens, the attack rate decreased by 23.08 percentage points, and the
mean number of deceased citizens decreased from 52.6 to 29.0. This
experiment indicates that, in the implemented spatial model,
vaccination becomes influential when access to vaccination points is
sufficiently broad.

\subsection{Hospital Experiment}
\label{sec:experiments-hospital}

Hospital coverage produced a substantial difference in health outcomes.
Without hospital support, the simulation produced 71.5 deceased citizens
and an attack rate of 66.5\% on average. Expanded hospital coverage,
consisting of three hospitals with a larger treatment radius and a
higher recovery bonus, reduced the mean number of deceased citizens to
26.1. Relative to the no-hospital scenario, this corresponds to 45.4
fewer deaths on average. The peak number of infected citizens also fell
from 50.2 to 26.6.

The observed effect is consistent with the implemented recovery
mechanism: infected citizens inside a non-full hospital area receive an
increased recovery probability. However, hospital placement also affects
movement, because infected citizens move towards nearby hospitals.
Consequently, this experiment evaluates the combined effect of improved
treatment coverage and hospital-seeking movement rather than the
recovery bonus in isolation.

\subsection{Quarantine Experiment}
\label{sec:experiments-quarantine}

In the baseline configuration, quarantine is activated only when at
least 100 citizens are actively infected. Since no baseline run reached
this threshold, quarantine was never activated. As a result, the
baseline and the explicitly disabled-quarantine scenario produced
identical results for every seed.

After decreasing the activation threshold to 20 infected citizens,
quarantine was activated in all ten runs, for 32.1 steps on average. The
mean peak number of infected citizens fell from 44.4 to 31.3, while the
attack rate fell from 56.8\% to 27.1\%. The mean number of deaths fell
from 51.8 to 23.7. The experiment therefore shows that the quarantine
mechanism can influence the epidemic, but the default threshold is too
high for it to become active in the baseline scenario.

\subsection{Avoidance Behavior and Interpretation Limits}
\label{sec:experiments-behavior}

The avoidance experiment produced a non-intuitive result. The scenario
with no avoidance behavior produced an attack rate of 47.4\%, while a
scenario in which 80\% of agents avoided visibly infected neighbors
produced an attack rate of 59.0\%. The high-avoidance scenario also
resulted in a slightly higher peak and more deaths than the baseline.

This observation should not be interpreted as a general claim that
avoidance increases epidemic spread. In the implemented model, avoiders
select a neighboring cell that does not currently contain a visibly
infected citizen. They do not avoid exposed citizens, and their movement
may lead several susceptible agents into similar locally safe cells.
Additionally, agents are activated sequentially in random order. The
result is therefore an emergent property of this specific movement rule
and indicates that the behavioral component should be refined and tested
further before drawing epidemiological conclusions.

More broadly, these experiments are descriptive simulation results rather
than statistical evidence for real-world intervention effectiveness. They
are based on ten stochastic repetitions of a simplified spatial model.
Nevertheless, they show that the developed framework correctly supports
controlled scenario analysis and that several implemented interventions
produce clear, measurable changes in the simulated epidemic trajectory.

\section{\SectionTitleSummary}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The objective of this project was to construct and evaluate an
agent-based simulation of epidemic spread in a heterogeneous population.
The developed system implements a spatial SEIRD model in which individual
citizens move on a grid, transmit infection through local contacts, and
may interact with hospitals, vaccination points, and quarantine
restrictions. The simulation also supports both aggregate visualization
of epidemic curves and a live spatial view of agent behavior.

The use of an agent-based approach is valuable in comparison with a
purely compartmental model when the research question concerns individual
behavior or spatial interventions. A standard SEIR-type model efficiently
describes changes in population totals, but it does not directly represent
where individuals are located, which hospital they can reach, whether
they enter a vaccination point, or how movement decisions modify local
contacts. In the developed system, these effects emerge from the actions
of individual agents. Therefore, the model provides a convenient
experimental framework for studying mechanisms that depend on local
interaction and heterogeneous behavior.

The experimental study consisted of 110 simulation runs distributed
across eleven scenarios. The baseline scenario produced an average attack
rate of 56.8\%, a mean epidemic peak of 44.4 active infections, and 51.8
deceased citizens. The results confirmed that the model reacts clearly to
changes in fundamental disease parameters: decreasing the transmission
probability substantially reduced both the epidemic extent and mortality,
while increasing it led to a larger outbreak.

The intervention experiments demonstrated that the spatial mechanisms
implemented in the model can have a measurable impact. A broadly
distributed and highly effective vaccination programme reduced the attack
rate from 56.6\% in the no-vaccination scenario to 33.6\%. Expanded
hospital coverage was particularly effective in the simulation, reducing
the average number of deceased citizens from 71.5 without hospital
support to 26.1. Early quarantine activation also strongly limited the
epidemic, reducing the attack rate from 56.8\% to 27.1\%. At the same
time, the experiments revealed that the default quarantine threshold was
too high: quarantine was never activated in baseline runs and thus had no
effect unless its threshold was changed.

\subsection{Conclusions from the Experiments}
\label{sec:conclusions-experiments}

The transmission experiment provides a useful reference for interpreting
the intervention scenarios. When the probability of transmission per
contact was reduced, the number of citizens exposed to the epidemic
decreased considerably: the attack rate fell from 56.8\% in the
baseline to 32.8\%, and the number of deaths fell from 51.8 to 29.5.
When transmission was increased, both values grew. This confirms that
the simulation follows the expected epidemic principle: reducing the
probability of successful transmission limits not only the number of
active infections but also the final health burden.

The hospital experiment yielded one of the clearest results. In this
model, a hospital does not prevent a susceptible citizen from becoming
infected; instead, it increases the recovery probability of an already
infected citizen located in its area. Nevertheless, this indirect
mechanism also influences the epidemic curve, because citizens who
recover sooner spend fewer steps in the infectious state. The scenario
without hospital support produced 71.5 deaths on average, whereas the
expanded hospital scenario produced 26.1 deaths, a reduction of 45.4
deaths, or approximately 63.5\%. The attack rate also fell from 66.5\%
to 37.2\%. Thus, within the simulated environment, accessible medical
care improves individual outcomes and indirectly reduces further
transmission by shortening infectious episodes.

The vaccination results show that effectiveness alone is not sufficient:
the intervention must also reach a meaningful fraction of the
population. With only the two default vaccination points, 24.3 citizens
were vaccinated on average and the result was nearly identical to the
scenario without vaccination. In contrast, the expanded programme
vaccinated 174.7 citizens on average and reduced the attack rate by
23.08 percentage points compared with no vaccination points. It also
reduced the mean number of deaths from 52.6 to 29.0. The conclusion is
that spatial accessibility is essential in this model: even an effective
vaccine has little population-level impact if agents rarely encounter a
vaccination point.

The quarantine experiment led to a similar practical conclusion. The
baseline configuration contained a quarantine mechanism, but its
activation threshold of 100 active infections was never reached.
Consequently, simply declaring that quarantine exists in the model does
not imply that it affects a simulation run. When the threshold was
lowered to 20, quarantine became active in every repetition and reduced
the attack rate from 56.8\% to 27.1\%, while deaths decreased from 51.8
to 23.7. This result indicates that the timing of an intervention is as
important as its presence: an intervention activated only after the
outbreak has already exceeded its typical scale cannot change the
baseline outcome.

The population in the model is heterogeneous with respect to age. Age
already influences the simulated outcomes in two ways: citizens above
the mobility threshold move less frequently, and mortality increases for
every year above the configured age threshold. Therefore, an older
population would be expected to be particularly sensitive to hospital
availability and epidemic prevention. However, the experimental series
reported in this work kept the age distribution constant across all
scenarios. The study therefore does not provide a measured comparison
between younger and older populations. A dedicated future experiment
should vary the age-bracket weights and compare mortality, hospital load,
and intervention effectiveness under aging-population scenarios.

An important conclusion is that the presence of a mechanism in the model
does not guarantee that it influences the results. The two default
vaccination points reached too few citizens to produce a substantial
difference from a scenario without vaccination. Similarly, the default
quarantine setting remained inactive. Scenario analysis was therefore
necessary not only to evaluate possible interventions, but also to verify
whether their configured parameters made them relevant in practice.

The behavioral experiment also highlighted a limitation of the current
implementation. Increasing the fraction of agents that avoided visibly
infected neighbors did not reduce the epidemic; in the tested setting it
produced a worse outcome than no avoidance behavior. This should be
understood as a property of the simplified movement algorithm rather than
as a conclusion about real human behavior. It indicates that behavioral
rules require careful calibration and validation before being used for
substantive interpretation.

The presented system should therefore be regarded as a functional
simulation and experimentation framework rather than a predictive model
of a specific real epidemic. Its advantages are transparency,
configurability, reproducibility through controlled seeds, and the
ability to visualize how aggregate curves emerge from local interactions.
Its limitations include simplified mobility, simplified hospital and
vaccination access, the absence of calibration against empirical data,
and a limited number of repetitions in the experimental study.

Further work could extend the model by adding more realistic contact
structures, such as households, schools, and workplaces; vaccination
strategies based on age or risk; explicit hospital admissions and
capacity queues; improved quarantine and isolation rules; and statistical
analysis over a larger number of simulation repetitions. It would also
be useful to refine the behavioral movement mechanism and test it against
well-defined expectations before drawing conclusions from avoidance-based
interventions.

In conclusion, the project shows that an agent-based model is a useful
tool for exploring epidemic dynamics when local interactions and
interventions matter. Although simplified, the implemented simulation
successfully supports controlled experiments and makes the effects of
transmission, vaccination, hospital coverage, and quarantine visible both
numerically and spatially.
